What Is a Business Model?

Reverse-engineering the Business Model Canvas through the company every student already knows: Airbnb.

2-hour session, English-taught Opens Phase 2: Business Models & Validation Bring: Week 4 personas & value propositions

Session overview

This session answers one question before students touch their own canvas: what is a business model, and what does a complete one actually look like? Instead of introducing the nine blocks in the abstract, the class reverse-engineers Airbnb block by block, then uses that model as a template before starting the team's own left-half draft. Teach it as a warm-up before the standard "left-half only" activity, or as an extended session if you want students to see the whole canvas before narrowing in on infrastructure.

Learning objectives

  • 1Define a business model in your own words — not just recite the textbook line.
  • 2Name all nine Business Model Canvas blocks and explain what each one is asking.
  • 3Trace how Airbnb's founding story became a two-sided platform business model.
  • 4Identify a hospitality or tourism example for each of the nine blocks, beyond Airbnb.

The case study

Airbnb, block by block

In 2008, co-founder Brian Chesky moved to San Francisco and struggled to make rent. When a design conference sold out every hotel in the city, he messaged his roommate Joe Gebbia with an idea: turn their apartment into a "Designer's Bed and Breakfast" for the event, offering out-of-town designers a place to crash for four days — Wi-Fi, a small desk, a sleeping mat, and breakfast each morning. Half-joking, they built a simple website overnight. Three strangers paid to stay with them that weekend. It didn't feel like a business yet. Six months later, Airbnb was born.

Tap each block below. For each, give the class the general idea first, ask the guiding question, then reveal Airbnb's answer. Work right to left — customer, then value, then infrastructure, then finance.

Key Partners +
Who you can't build alone

Companies partner to gain scale, reduce risk, or acquire a resource they lack in-house.

Who did Airbnb need early on that it couldn't build itself?

Payment processors for secure transactions, and later, local photographers — added after testing showed professional photos roughly doubled booking rates.

Key Activities +
What you must repeatedly do

Production, problem-solving, or platform-building are the three common types.

What does the company have to keep doing every day?

Growing and supporting the host community, and continuously building features based on what hosts and guests need — platform-type activity.

Key Resources +
What you must own or control

Physical, intellectual, human, or financial resources.

Airbnb owns no real estate — so what does it own?

Brand trust, the host and guest community itself, and investor backing.

Value Propositions +
The pain relieved, or gain created

Not the product itself — the reason the customer won't leave.

What does each side actually get?

Travelers: a home away from home, and a local way to experience a city. Hosts: extra income from space they already own.

Customer Relationships +
How close, or how self-service

Ranges from low-touch self-service to high-touch personal assistance.

How does Airbnb build trust between strangers?

Host communities sharing tips, and constant iteration from feedback — like adding neighborhood info because guests cared about local context.

Channels +
Awareness, purchase, delivery

Covers the whole journey, not just where you sell.

How do people find and decide to trust Airbnb?

Direct app and website bookings, plus word-of-mouth — good stays get shared, bringing in new users for free.

Customer Segments +
Who you serve, who you don't

Mass, niche, segmented, or diversified markets.

Airbnb has two very different user groups — who?

A two-sided platform: travelers looking for a stay, and hosts with space to rent — each with its own value, channel, and revenue treatment.

Cost Structure +
Fixed vs. variable, cost- vs. value-driven

Where the money goes, and whether the model competes on price or on value.

Where does most of Airbnb's money actually go?

Mostly marketing and technology, plus partner costs like the photography program — a value-driven model, spending to improve trust and experience.

Revenue Streams +
Why, and how, the customer pays

Usage fees, subscriptions, rental fees, or licensing.

Airbnb owns no property — so how does it make money?

A service fee charged to both sides of every transaction, host and guest — what makes the two-sided model self-sustaining.

The "aha" moment to highlight Key Partners here isn't just supply-chain logic — the photographer partnership came from testing a hypothesis, then building an activity around the answer. A good bridge into Week 8's market research theme.
Common mistake to flag Students often list "guests" or "hosts" as Key Partners. Customers are never a Key Partner, even in a two-sided model — real partners are the third parties who help deliver the value, not the customers themselves.

Hour 2 · Activity

Sketch your own full canvas

Working right to left, sketch a rough answer for all nine blocks — one sentence per block is enough for a first draft.

  1. Start from your Value Proposition (Week 4) and your two customer personas.
  2. Fill in all nine blocks — avoid vague answers like "everyone" or plain "marketing."
  3. Use the Airbnb example as a model for how specific your answers should be.
  4. Circle any block your team is unsure about — it gets attention in Week 7.

Same story, later in the semester

Extending the Airbnb thread

Week 1 seed

From an air mattress to a global company

Used as a mindset hook before students know any Canvas vocabulary. Chesky's specific pitch to Gebbia — Wi-Fi, a desk, a sleeping mat, breakfast, for one type of guest — previews what a value proposition looks like, and the scrappy weekend fix previews the idea of an MVP, both without naming the terms yet.

Week 12 harvest

The $40 box of cereal that funded Airbnb

The same founders return to teach fixed vs. variable costs and value-based pricing: novelty election cereal that cost a few dollars to make, priced at $40, raised about $30,000 — and convinced Y Combinator's Paul Graham to fund them.
